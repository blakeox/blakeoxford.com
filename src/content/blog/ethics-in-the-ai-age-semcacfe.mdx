This was not an AI hype talk. The promise was practical: leave with a usable risk lens, and language you can put in a policy, around judgment, evidence quality, confidentiality, and professional responsibility.

## Leverage is real. Evidence standards do not change.

AI helps surface duplicate vendors, unusual journal entries, contract summaries, and side-agreement signals. Before you allege vendor fraud, you still need documents, approvals, payments, bank data, and ownership links. A risk score is not a fraud conclusion.

For agentic workflows, approval gates and access limits are how you keep a professional opinion defensible.

## What attendees can actually use

1. **Right-size governance.** Small firms still need a simple version — ownership, approved use cases, testing, and escalation — not a binder nobody reads.
2. **Borrow from NIST AI RMF.** Govern, Map, Measure, Manage translates cleanly into firm policy language.
3. **Treat material AI use like workpapers.** Keep prompts that shaped the analysis; preserve outputs you relied on. If you would not explain it in deposition, do not build your opinion on it.
4. **Ask hard vendor questions.** If a tool cannot answer who can access data, how outputs are logged, and how the model was evaluated for your use case, it is not ready for sensitive professional work.

## The point is not to avoid AI

The job is to use the tool in a way the profession can defend — verify, document, and be ready to explain the method. That same evidence discipline shows up in work like [grounding legal AI in real court data](/blog/combating-legal-ai-hallucinations/).
